# The speech — word for word

Short sentences on purpose. Say them slowly. Pause where it says pause.
Total: about 2 minutes 50 seconds. Practise twice out loud before you go up.

**Before you start:** have the tool already open on screen, showing London.
Do not open it in front of the room. Have it ready.

---

### 1. Open — 20 seconds

> "There are children in London tonight who have never had a bedroom that stayed theirs
> for a whole school year.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> Right now, one hundred thousand, nine hundred and thirty children in London live in
> temporary accommodation. Hotel rooms. Hostels. Not homes.
>
> That is the highest number ever recorded. Most people in this room are still quoting
> eighty-five thousand. That figure is out of date. London passed one hundred thousand,
> and nobody announced it."

---

### 2. The money — 35 seconds

> "This is also a money problem.
>
> Last year, London boroughs spent seven hundred and forty million pounds on temporary
> accommodation. Three years ago it was two hundred and forty three million. It has tripled.
>
> *(point at the red and green chart)*
>
> Red is temporary accommodation. Green is prevention — the money that stops the next
> family needing help. Red is running away. Green is flat.
>
> Temporary accommodation now takes seventy-one percent of all homelessness spending in
> London. In Newham it is eighty-eight percent.
>
> And these are not our estimates. These are the numbers the councils themselves reported
> to the government."

---

### 3. The waste — 25 seconds

> "Now the other side.
>
> In five years, London gave planning permission for three hundred and twenty thousand homes.
> It finished one hundred and eighty four thousand.
>
> One hundred and twelve thousand homes were never even started. Not delayed. Never started.
>
> *(pause)*
>
> So London is paying for hotel rooms, and not building the homes it already said yes to."

---

### 4. The twist — 40 seconds  ← this is the part that wins

> "Here is where we were wrong.
>
> We assumed the boroughs with the worst temporary accommodation crisis would be the same
> boroughs failing to build. The hypocrisy story. We tested it across thirty-one boroughs.
>
> *(scroll to the scatter plot)*
>
> There is no relationship. None. Knowing how bad a borough's crisis is tells you nothing
> about how many homes it left unbuilt.
>
> Look at the two ends. Barnet has worse than average pressure, and it still started more
> homes than it approved. Greenwich has less pressure than average, and thirteen thousand
> approved homes that never broke ground.
>
> So this is not councils being lazy. It is worse. **London is building in the wrong places.**
> The permissions are real. They are just not where the people are."

---

### 5. The demo — 30 seconds

> "So we built this for anyone to check.
>
> *(ask the room)* Somebody shout out a borough.
>
> *(click that borough on the map — wait for it to load)*
>
> There. Households. Children. How many were sent outside the borough. What it costs.
> How much of the budget it eats.
>
> *(point at the button)*
>
> And a ready-written email to your councillor, with your own borough's numbers already in it.
>
> You can type a postcode too. It works offline, in one file, on any laptop."

---

### 6. The answer — 25 seconds

> "Because the homes are in the wrong places, we can say where to start.
>
> *(scroll to 'So build here first')*
>
> Six boroughs where the need is worst **and** the permission already exists.
> Tower Hamlets. Newham. Enfield. Southwark. Haringey. Wandsworth.
>
> Together: thirty-seven thousand approved homes that were never started.
> Twenty-four thousand families in temporary accommodation.
>
> The permission to house every one of those families, one and a half times over,
> has already been granted. Nobody has to approve anything new."

---

### 7. Honesty — 20 seconds  ← do not skip this

> "Three things we could be wrong about.
>
> One. Unbuilt does not prove land banking. Some of it is normal building time.
>
> Two. Our correlation is a null result. With thirty-one boroughs we could only find a
> strong relationship. We are saying we see no link, not that there is none.
>
> Three. We could not measure how long these permissions have been sitting, because the
> only site-level data is frozen at 2020. That is our third ask: publish it."

---

### 8. Close — 15 seconds

> "Every number here is open government data. No agreements, nothing hidden.
> The whole thing rebuilds with one command, so next quarter it updates itself.
>
> The data to argue for fixing this already existed.
> We just made it something you can type your own postcode into.
>
> Thank you."

---

## If something breaks

- **Wifi dies:** you already have `dist/homes-vs-hotels.html` on the laptop. Double-click it.
  Everything works offline except the postcode box.
- **A borough looks odd:** some boroughs built more than they approved. That is real, not a bug.
  Say: "that one is actually delivering — it is working through older permissions."
- **You lose your place:** go back to the six boroughs. That is the ending. Land it and stop.

## The four questions you will get

**"A null result isn't a finding."**
> "It is when the whole room assumes the opposite. The default story is that councils cry
> poverty while sitting on permissions. At borough level that is not supported. And it changes
> what you would actually do — target the pipeline instead of shaming the councils."

**"Where did the cost numbers come from?"**
> "Form RO4 of the government's revenue outturn. What each council reported it actually spent.
> We use net, not gross, because housing benefit covers most of the gross figure. Net is what
> lands on the borough's own budget."

**"Isn't the unbuilt gap just normal building time?"**
> "Partly, for the completions gap, and we say so. But one hundred and twelve thousand homes
> never broke ground at all. Build time does not explain a site that never started."

**"Hillingdon at 287% of reserves — is it bankrupt?"**
> "No, and we do not claim that. It pays from revenue, not reserves. It is a measure of how
> big the bill has become compared to the money it could reach for in an emergency."
