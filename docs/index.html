What should change

  1. Target build-out support at these six boroughs. They are where acute need and unstarted permissions already coincide. Nothing here requires a new planning decision — the consent exists. This is the one intervention the mismatch finding actually supports, because London's problem is not a shortage of permissions but their location.
  2. Put a floor under prevention spending. Temporary accommodation has gone from 56% to 71% of London's homelessness budget in three years. Every pound it takes is a pound not spent stopping the next family needing it, which is what makes this a loop rather than a line.
  3. Publish time-since-permission on unstarted sites. We could not tell you how long these 112,104 homes have been sitting, because the only site-level source is frozen at a 2020 extract. Without it nobody can distinguish a scheme waiting on finance from one being sat on, and every land-banking argument stays an argument.

How this was made

The data

Temporary accommodation figures come from MHCLG's statutory homelessness detailed local-authority tables (table TA1), the official quarterly return every English council files. Nothing there is modelled or estimated: household counts, children, bed-and-breakfast placements and out-of-borough placements are as published. Figures are snapshots at the end of each December, so seasonal comparison is like-for-like.

Approvals and completions come from the GLA's London Plan Annual Monitoring Report 21 (January 2026), chapter 2, covering the five years 2019/20 to 2023/24 — the most recent published window.

Spending comes from MHCLG's local authority revenue outturn, form RO4 (housing services), which is what each council actually reported it spent. Temporary accommodation is the sum of the six placement categories; prevention is the Homelessness Reduction Act administration, prevention, relief and support line.

What we're less sure about
  • The approvals gap is not the same as land banking. This is the single biggest caveat on the page. Homes approved in 2023/24 could not physically have been completed by 2023/24, so some of the gap is simply build-out time rather than anything stalling. It is a measure of consent running ahead of delivery across a fixed five-year window, not a count of sites deliberately sat on. Separating the two needs site-level permission dates from the London Development Database, which is the obvious next build.
  • Two development corporations are excluded. The Old Oak and Park Royal and London Legacy corporations are the planning authority for parts of several boroughs, so consents inside their areas are missing from those boroughs' figures and their homes are counted in the London total only.
  • Spending is net current expenditure, not gross. Gross temporary accommodation costs are far larger but are substantially offset by housing benefit subsidy. Net is what a council actually carries on its own budget, which is the figure that drives financial distress. Only the build cost in the comparison above is an assumption, and it is a slider.
  • The mismatch finding is a null result, and null results are weak evidence. With 31 boroughs we could only detect a strong relationship; a modest one would hide in the noise. What we can say is that there is no obvious link, not that we have proved none exists. The correlation is on ranks, with a permutation p-value, because 31 points do not justify distributional assumptions.
  • "Spendable reserves" is our definition, not a statutory one. It is unallocated reserves plus other earmarked reserves at the year end, excluding schools, dedicated schools grant and public health, which are ring-fenced and cannot be spent on homelessness. Comparing an annual cost to a one-off stock of reserves is a rough gauge of exposure, not an accounting statement — a borough spending more than its reserves each year is not thereby insolvent, it is funding the cost from revenue.
  • The spending series starts at 2021-22. Earlier revenue outturn releases exist, but most boroughs leave the homelessness component lines blank before then, so a longer run would compare against gaps rather than against genuinely smaller numbers.
  • Two boroughs did not return usable data in December 2019, so their change figure is measured from the first year they did. The baseline year is labelled per borough.
  • A household is not a fixed number of people. Household and children counts move differently, and neither is a count of individuals in TA.
  • Out-of-borough placements are counted by the placing borough, so a borough exporting families looks worse on that measure and the receiving borough does not appear at all.
What would change our conclusion

The claim on this page is narrow: TA caseloads in London are at a record high and rising, boroughs are absorbing that as a recurring revenue cost, and consent is running well ahead of delivery at the same time. It would be wrong if the TA rise were driven by better recording rather than more households — worth testing against the underlying homelessness duty-acceptance series — or if boroughs had materially shifted placements into cheaper council-owned stock, which the accommodation-type split in the same table lets you check. On the supply side, it would be substantially weakened if the approvals gap turned out to be normal build-out lag rather than delivery failure, which is why the two numbers are shown side by side rather than presented as cause and effect.
